Proof of Reason

Reserves prove the money is there. Reason proves why it moved.

What it is

Some companies manage other people's money in shared pools, and they are starting to let AI decide where that money goes. Before the AI moves any of it, Proof of Reason makes it explain why, checks whether the explanation is actually true right now, and only lets the money move if it is. Every check is written down in public so anyone can see it later.

Why a manager wants it

Because AI gets things wrong, gets tricked, and cannot be cross-examined. If the AI says this place pays 12 percent and the money can come back any time, and that is false, the money does not move. And when a customer asks why their money went there, the manager can show a record written by someone independent, not their own notes.

Why a customer or an insurer wants it

They can see why the money moved without having to trust the manager or the AI. A bank publishes proof that deposits exist. This proves why deposits moved.

What it checks, every time

Three questions. Is what the AI said true? Is the move something the owner allowed? Did the AI give every reason it is required to give? Three yeses, the money moves. One no, it does not, and the refusal is on the record too.

What it does not do

It does not judge whether the move was a good idea. It judges whether the reasons for it were true and allowed. That is the whole job.

The first check, 25 September 2026

Our own test AI asked to move 5 dollars between two lending markets. It gave two reasons. Both were true at that moment. The move was allowed. The money moved seven seconds later, 20 blocks on the chain. This was our own AI and does not count as a customer.

  • It said this market pays 9.5558 percent. The market read 9.5558 percent. True.
  • It said at least 1,639,330.385558 dollars could be taken out on demand. The market read 1,821,478.206176 dollars. True.
  • Allowed: both markets it touched were on the list the owner had approved.
  • Complete: both kinds of reason it was required to give were there.
  • Result: three yeses, at block 107991649. The money moved seven seconds later, 20 blocks on the chain.

The failure it is built for

In the fourteen months to March 2026, reporting counts at least four cases of the same failure: a lending market trusted a price that was hard-set or had stopped updating, while the real market price had moved. Usual, January 2025: a token hard-set at one dollar; when its redemption floor was cut to 87 cents, lenders were locked in. Stream, November 2025: the price stopped updating as its token collapsed, and an estimated 285 to 700 million dollars was at risk; another outlet reported ten times that. Moonwell, October and November 2025: two price failures, over 5 million dollars of bad debt. Resolv, March 2026: a token marked at 1.13 dollars while it traded near 0.63; one lender absorbed over 10 million dollars of bad debt in a single day.

Today the check reads rates and the cash available to withdraw. Reading the price a lender uses for its collateral is not built yet.

Doctrine

The LLM reads. The chain testifies. TypeScript decides.

Hold the veto, never the keys.

How it works

  1. The agent states its reason as typed claims.
  2. The validator reads chain state at a pinned block and checks every premise.
  3. A signed verdict, PASS, WAIT or FAIL, is written to an ERC-8004 validation registry.
  4. The gate executes only on PASS, asks the owner on WAIT, and reverts on FAIL.

What it does not do: it does not judge whether a decision is wise, only whether its premises are true and allowed.

What it catches

  • A false premise. Stream Finance, November 2025: a hardcoded $1.00 oracle against a $0.26 market, $285M exposed. The TRUE check reads the price at the block.
  • A disallowed action. Grok/Bankr, May 2026: a prompt-injected permission grant, $174k out. The ALLOWED check holds the mandate.

Most agent gates check permissions. Proof of Reason checks premises.

Why Monad

A verdict is only useful if execution follows before the state it checked moves. The gate accepts a verdict for 60 blocks, about 18 seconds at Monad's 0.30 s block time. The first check to execution took 20 blocks, 7 seconds by block timestamps. The same 60-block bound on Ethereum L1 is 12 minutes.

Monad charges a transaction its gas limit, not its gas used, so a refused call costs the same as an executed one. The gate exposes a free read, previewPremises, that returns the same chain state the gate will re-check, and the agent asks it before spending a limit. Monad prices the first read of a storage page and warms the rest, and a Morpho market's state sits on one page, so re-asserting a utilization or liquidity premise inside the executing transaction is one cheap read rather than a reason to trust the validator.

Known limits

  • One validator key today; every verdict is on the registry and re-checkable by anyone, and a second validator is the roadmap.
  • What the gate still trusts the validator for: the observations the chain cannot re-read, rates above all, at the pinned block, and that the signed tally counts what the receipt shows. Both are checkable afterwards against an archive node. Utilization, liquidity and the Kuru book are re-read by the chain at execution, and the rule that turns counts into an outcome is compiled into the gate.
  • The validator sees an agent's calldata and claims before the chain does. A dishonest validator could trade on that. Today the answer is a policy, not a mechanism: no copy of non-PASS intents beyond their receipts, and no trading on gate inputs. A second validator or an enclave would replace the policy.
  • The check judges premises and mandate, not whether the decision is wise.
  • The registry is interim until Monad's canonical ERC-8004 validation registry ships.
  • The 30-day audit reads MetaMorpho V1 vaults today. Morpho Vault V2, used by some curators, is not supported yet.
